# Banned AI-Tell Patterns

This reference covers the words, phrases, structures, and formatting habits that make text read like it came from a language model. If you spot any of these in your output, fix them before the reader sees it.

Why does this matter? Readers have developed a sixth sense for machine-generated text. Certain words and patterns show up so often in AI output that they've become fingerprints. Avoiding them isn't about hiding anything -- it's about writing clearly and sounding like a real person.

## Tier 2 -- Banned phrases

These phrases are filler. They pad sentences without adding meaning, and most of them are verbal tics that language models picked up from corporate writing. Cut them or replace them with something concrete.

- **"in order to"** -- to

The pattern here is simple: if a phrase delays the actual content, it doesn't belong. Good writing gets to the point. That doesn't mean you can't set context -- just do it with specifics, not with throat-clearing.

## Tier 3 -- Banned structural patterns

AI-generated text has tells that go beyond word choice. The shape of the writing gives it away too: predictable rhythms, symmetrical structures, neat little packages. Real human writing is messier. Embrace the mess.

Watch for these:

- Exactly 3-item or 5-item lists as the default. Language models love threes and fives. If your list lands on 3 or 5 items, either add one, remove one, or rewrite it as prose.
- Lists that announce their count ("There are three key benefits"). Don't tell readers how many items are coming -- just show them.
- Every paragraph opening with a topic sentence. Vary your openings. Start some paragraphs with a question, an example, a consequence, a data point, or a direct claim.
- Consecutive paragraphs with identical internal structure. If paragraph A goes claim-evidence-implication and paragraph B does the same, rewrite one of them. Mix it up.
- Sentences that all follow subject-verb-object without variation. English is flexible -- use fronted clauses, inversions, fragments, interjections.
- Closing paragraphs that begin with transition phrases announcing a conclusion. "In conclusion," "To summarize," "In summary," "Overall," and "Ultimately" all scream machine-generated. End with a new insight, a next step, or an implication instead. <!-- voice-ok -->
- Opening paragraphs that begin with broad context-setting. "In the world of..." and "When it comes to..." are stalling tactics. Start with something specific and interesting.

Here's a quick test: read your text aloud. Does it sound like a speech? Like someone presenting slides? That's the structural monotony showing. Real writing has rhythm changes, unexpected turns, moments where the pace shifts. If every section reads like a five-paragraph essay, something's wrong.

## Tier 4 -- Banned formatting patterns

Formatting choices can be AI tells too. These show up often enough in generated text that readers notice them.

- More than 2 em-dashes per section. Em-dashes are great, but AI overuses them. Reach for semicolons, colons, parentheses, or just split the sentence.
- Bold on every key term. Bold is for genuine emphasis -- the thing you'd stress if you were speaking. When everything's bold, nothing is.
- Headers that are questions when a statement would work. "What is authentication?" reads like a FAQ bot. "Authentication" or "How authentication works" reads like a human organizing their thoughts.
- Excessive use of "Note:" or "Important:" callouts. One per page, maximum. If everything's a note, you're just writing paragraphs with extra formatting.
- Parenthetical asides in every paragraph. One or two per section is natural. More than that and it feels like the writer can't commit to what's main content and what's secondary.

The goal with formatting is restraint. Let the words do the work. Formatting should help readers scan and find things; it shouldn't be doing the persuading.
